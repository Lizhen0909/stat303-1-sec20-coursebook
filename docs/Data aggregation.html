12  Data Grouping and Aggregation

The last two chapters used groupby() in its simplest form: one grouping column, one statistic, such as a continent’s mean GDP to fill a gap in Data Cleaning or a spending tertile’s mean graduation rate in Data Preparation. That one line is an instance of a general pattern, and most summaries in data analysis follow it: split the rows into groups, compute something within each group, and combine the results into a table.

This chapter makes that pattern a full tool. It covers counting by category, computing several statistics at once with readable names, grouping by more than one column, and the three things you can do with groups besides summarizing them: give every row a group-level value, keep or drop whole groups, and take a few rows from each. Along the way it asks a question every grouped summary raises: what exactly is being averaged?

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Count rows by category with value_counts(), crosstab(), and groupby().size(), and count distinct values with nunique().
  • Describe groupby() as split–apply–combine, and summarize each group with one statistic, several statistics via agg(), or named results via named aggregation.
  • Group by two columns, read and select from the resulting two-level index, and turn it back into ordinary columns with reset_index().
  • Decide whether a group mean should weight every row equally or by a size column such as population, and compute a weighted mean by group.
  • Use transform() to give every row a group-level value, filter() to keep or drop whole groups, and head() or sample() to take rows from each group.

12.3 Count by category

The simplest summary of a group is its size. Three tools count, and they differ in what they count and how many columns they cross.

value_counts(), from Pandas Fundamentals, counts the rows in each category of one column:

gdp["continent"].value_counts()
continent
Africa      624
Asia        396
Europe      360
Americas    300
Oceania      24
Name: count, dtype: int64

These are rows, not countries: Africa has 624 rows because 52 countries are each recorded in 12 years. To count distinct countries, use nunique(), the number of unique values, within each continent:

gdp.groupby("continent")["country"].nunique()
continent
Africa      52
Americas    25
Asia        33
Europe      30
Oceania      2
Name: country, dtype: int64

Oceania is only 2 countries, Australia and New Zealand. Any comparison involving “Oceania” is a comparison with two countries, which is worth knowing before reading a summary table.

crosstab(), which drew the island-by-species counts in Data Visualization I, counts combinations of two columns. margins=True adds row and column totals labelled All:

pd.crosstab(gdp["continent"], gdp["year"], margins=True)

continent
Africa 52 52 52 52 52 52 52 52 52 52 52 52 624
Americas 25 25 25 25 25 25 25 25 25 25 25 25 300
Asia 33 33 33 33 33 33 33 33 33 33 33 33 396
Europe 30 30 30 30 30 30 30 30 30 30 30 30 360
Oceania 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 24

Every continent has the same number of rows in every year, so the data is balanced: no country drops in or out over time. That is worth checking, because a continent whose countries appear only in later years would show a “trend” made by the changing membership rather than by change within countries.

Question Tool
How many rows in each category? df["col"].value_counts()
How many distinct values in each group? df.groupby("g")["col"].nunique()
How many rows in each combination of two columns? pd.crosstab(df["a"], df["b"])

12.4 Split, apply, combine

Counting is one summary per group. Most questions need others — a mean, a median, a total — and the tool that computes any of them by group is groupby().

Why group and aggregate?

  • Summarize a large table. Thousands of rows become one row per group that a reader can take in: total sales by region instead of every transaction.
  • Compare categories. Patterns become visible when groups sit side by side: outcomes by treatment group, graduation rates by type of college, life expectancy by continent.
  • Follow groups over time. Grouping by a time period — month, quarter, year — turns a stream of records into a trend you can plot.
  • Reduce complexity. A summary at the right level answers the question without the noise of every individual row.

Every one of these works the same way. groupby() follows three steps, a pattern known as split–apply–combine:

  1. Split the rows into groups, one per distinct value of the grouping column.
  2. Apply a computation to each group separately.
  3. Combine the results into one Series or DataFrame, labelled by group.

The diagram’s six rows, as a DataFrame, give exactly its result:

toy = pd.DataFrame({"ID": ["X", "Y", "Z", "X", "Y", "Z"],
                    "Values": [10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60]})
toy.groupby("ID")["Values"].sum()
ID
X    50
Y    70
Z    90
Name: Values, dtype: int64

Split puts rows with the same ID together (X holds 10 and 40), apply sums each group, and combine stacks the three sums into one Series labelled by ID: X = 50, Y = 70, Z = 90. The same three steps run on the GDP data, only with more rows.

gdp.groupby("continent") does only the first step. It returns a GroupBy object that records which rows belong to which group but computes nothing until you ask:

by_continent = gdp.groupby("continent")
by_continent.size()

Oceania      24

12.5 Summarize with agg()

One statistic per group is often not enough. GDP per capita in 2007 shows why: a mean alone hides how unequal the countries within a continent are. The rest of this section works with that single year:

gdp_2007 = gdp[gdp["year"] == 2007]
gdp_2007.shape
(142, 6)

agg() takes a list of statistics and returns one column for each:

gdp_2007.groupby("continent")["gdpPercap"].agg(["mean", "median", "max"]).round(0)
mean median max
continent
Africa 3089.0 1452.0 13206.0
Americas 11003.0 8948.0 42952.0
Asia 12473.0 4471.0 47307.0
Europe 25054.0 28054.0 49357.0
Oceania 29810.0 29810.0 34435.0

In Asia the mean, about $12,500, is almost three times the median, about $4,500. Half of Asian countries are below $4,500, and a few very rich ones pull the mean up: the same right skew as expenditure in Data Cleaning. In Europe the median is above the mean, because a handful of poorer countries pull the mean down. Reporting both shows what one number alone would hide.

12.5.1 Named aggregation

When the summary is going into a report, give each result a name. Named aggregation passes one argument per output column, written as name=(column, statistic). It also lets each output come from a different column:

summary_2007 = gdp_2007.groupby("continent").agg(
    countries=("country", "nunique"),
    median_gdp=("gdpPercap", "median"),
    mean_life=("lifeExp", "mean"),
    total_pop=("pop", "sum"),
)
summary_2007.round(1)
countries median_gdp mean_life total_pop
continent
Africa 52 1452.3 54.8 929539692
Americas 25 8948.1 73.6 898871184
Asia 33 4471.1 70.7 3811953827
Europe 30 28054.1 77.6 586098529
Oceania 2 29810.2 80.7 24549947

Each argument name becomes a column. The result reads like the table you would put in a report, and the code says exactly what each number is.

12.5.2 Your own statistic

A statistic that pandas does not provide can be written as a function, and agg() will apply it to each group. A lambda, from Pandas Intermediate, is the short form. The range of GDP per capita within each continent:

gdp_2007.groupby("continent").agg(
    lowest_gdp=("gdpPercap", "min"),
    highest_gdp=("gdpPercap", "max"),
    gdp_range=("gdpPercap", lambda x: x.max() - x.min()),
).round(0)
lowest_gdp highest_gdp gdp_range
continent
Africa 278.0 13206.0 12929.0
Americas 1202.0 42952.0 41750.0
Asia 944.0 47307.0 46363.0
Europe 5937.0 49357.0 43420.0
Oceania 25185.0 34435.0 9250.0

The lambda receives each continent’s gdpPercap values as a Series x and returns one number. The same works for any rule you can write in a line: a percentile with lambda x: x.quantile(0.9), or the share above a threshold with lambda x: (x > 10_000).mean().

Goal Code
One statistic df.groupby("g")["col"].mean()
Several statistics for one column df.groupby("g")["col"].agg(["mean", "median"])
Named results, any columns df.groupby("g").agg(name=("col", "mean"), ...)
Your own statistic name=("col", lambda x: x.max() - x.min())

Every continent gained, and Africa’s gain stalled from the late 1980s to the early 2000s while the others kept rising. Turning a grouped Series into a wide table, with one column per year, is reshaping, which is the subject of the next chapter.

12.7 What a group mean averages

Each value in that plot is a mean over countries: Mongolia and China count equally toward Asia’s figure. That answers the question “what was life expectancy in a typical Asian country?” It does not answer “how long did a typical person in Asia live?”, because China and India hold most of Asia’s people.

The second question needs a weighted mean, in which each country counts in proportion to its population:

\[\text{weighted mean} = \frac{\sum (\text{pop} \times \text{lifeExp})}{\sum \text{pop}}\]

groupby() can compute both sums at once. Make a column of the products, sum it and pop by group, and divide:

gdp["pop_x_life"] = gdp["pop"] * gdp["lifeExp"]
sums = gdp.groupby(["continent", "year"])[["pop_x_life", "pop"]].sum()
weighted_life = sums["pop_x_life"] / sums["pop"]

asia = pd.DataFrame({
    "per country (unweighted)": life_by_year.loc["Asia"],
    "per person (weighted by population)": weighted_life.loc["Asia"],
}).round(1)
asia.loc[[1952, 1982, 2007]]
per country (unweighted) per person (weighted by population)
year
1952 46.3 42.9
1982 62.6 61.6
2007 70.7 69.4

In 1952 the typical Asian country had a life expectancy of 46.3 years, but the typical Asian person only 42.9, because the most populous countries had some of the lowest values. Dividing two group sums keeps everything aligned by the shared continent–year index, so each weighted mean uses its own group’s numbers.

Plotting both versions over every year shows something the three rows hide. asia.plot(), the pandas plotting from Data Visualization I, draws one line per column against the index:

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.5), layout="constrained")
asia.plot(ax=ax, marker="o")
ax.set(xlabel="Year", ylabel="Life expectancy in Asia (years)",
       title="Per person, Asia's life expectancy fell in 1962; per country, it rose")
plt.show()

Between 1957 and 1962 the per-country line keeps rising, but the per-person line falls, from 47.3 to 46.6 years, and the gap between them jumps to 5 years. The cause is one country. China’s life expectancy dropped from 50.5 years in 1957 to 44.5 in 1962, the years of the famine of 1959–1961, and China held 39% of Asia’s people. In a per-country mean China is one country in 33, so the drop barely registers; weighted by population, it dominates.

Neither mean is wrong; they answer different questions. When you report a group mean, say which one it is — per country, per person, per college — because the reader cannot tell from the number.

12.8 Give every row a group value: transform()

Summaries return one row per group. Sometimes you want the group value on every row instead, to compare each row with its own group. Data Cleaning used transform("mean") to fill a missing GDP with its continent’s mean; the same tool answers “how does each country compare with its continent in the same year?”:

continent_year_mean = gdp.groupby(["continent", "year"])["gdpPercap"].transform("mean")
gdp["gdp_vs_continent"] = gdp["gdpPercap"] / continent_year_mean

gdp.loc[gdp["year"] == 2007,
        ["country", "continent", "gdpPercap", "gdp_vs_continent"]].sort_values(
    "gdp_vs_continent", ascending=False).head().round(2)
country continent gdpPercap gdp_vs_continent
551 Gabon Africa 13206.48 4.28
167 Botswana Africa 12569.85 4.07
491 Equatorial Guinea Africa 12154.09 3.93
1619 United States Americas 42951.65 3.90
911 Libya Africa 12057.50 3.90

transform("mean") returns 1,704 values, one per row in the original order, so it can be divided into gdpPercap directly. A value of 2 means twice the continent’s mean in that year. Any statistic name works in transform(), as does a lambda.

The table says Gabon’s GDP per capita in 2007 was 4.28 times Africa’s mean: oil revenue spread over a small population, in a continent where the typical economy is far poorer. Four of the top five are African for the same reason. A few such countries are also what puts Africa’s mean GDP per capita ($3,089 in 2007) at about twice its median ($1,452).

12.9 Keep or drop whole groups: filter()

filter() tests each group against a rule and keeps all the rows of the groups that pass. The rule is a function that receives one group as a DataFrame and returns True or False. Which countries gained more than 30 years of life expectancy between 1952 and 2007?

big_gainers = gdp.groupby("country").filter(
    lambda g: g["lifeExp"].max() - g["lifeExp"].min() > 30
)
print(big_gainers["country"].nunique(), "countries")
big_gainers["country"].unique()
9 countries
<StringArray>
[         'Indonesia',        'Korea, Rep.',              'Libya',
          'Nicaragua',               'Oman',       'Saudi Arabia',
            'Vietnam', 'West Bank and Gaza',        'Yemen, Rep.']
Length: 9, dtype: str

Every row of those 9 countries is kept, all 12 years each, so the result can be plotted or summarized like the full data. A Boolean mask on rows, by contrast, tests each row on its own:

over_70 = gdp[gdp["lifeExp"] > 70]
print(len(over_70), "rows from", over_70["country"].nunique(), "countries")
493 rows from 84 countries

The mask keeps every country-year above 70, so most of those 84 countries appear only for their later years. Use a mask for a rule about a row, and filter() for a rule about a group as a whole.

12.10 Take rows from each group: head() and sample()

To take the top rows of each group, sort first, then take head() within each group:

(gdp[gdp["year"] == 2007]
    .sort_values("lifeExp", ascending=False)
    .groupby("continent")
    .head(2)[["continent", "country", "lifeExp"]])
continent country lifeExp
803 Asia Japan 82.603
671 Asia Hong Kong, China 82.208

1487 Europe Switzerland 81.701

That is the two longest-lived countries of each continent in 2007. The expression is wrapped in parentheses so that each step can sit on its own line.

sample() draws rows at random within each group. A plain random sample of the whole table reflects the continents’ sizes, so Oceania, with 24 rows, would barely appear. A stratified sample takes the same number from every group instead:

stratified = gdp.groupby("continent").sample(n=24, random_state=303)
stratified["continent"].value_counts()
continent
Africa      24
Americas    24
Asia        24
Europe      24
Oceania     24
Name: count, dtype: int64

random_state=303 fixes the random draw, so the sample is the same every time the cell runs. Here n=24 takes every Oceania row, since that is all there is.

Beyond summaries.

Goal Code
Group value on every row df.groupby("g")["col"].transform("mean")

The rule to remember: before you report a group summary, know how many rows and distinct units are in each group, and what each one counts for.

12.14 Before You Move On

The habit to carry forward is knowing what a group summary counts. A count of rows is not a count of units; a mean over countries is not a mean over people; a continent with two countries is not comparable, on equal terms, with a continent of fifty. None of these produces an error, and every one changes what a summary table says.

The other habit is choosing the operation by the shape of the result you need: one row per group to summarize, every row with a group value to compare, whole groups to keep or drop, or a few rows from each group to sample.

For any grouped summary you make or read, you should be able to answer five questions:

  1. What are the groups, and how many rows and distinct units does each contain?
  2. Which statistic summarizes each group, and why that one?
  3. Does each row count equally, and should it?
  4. Is the data balanced across groups and over time?
  5. Did you need one row per group, or a group value on every row?
